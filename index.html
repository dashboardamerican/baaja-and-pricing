Sources & method

What the two numbers mean

For each year, B₀ is the inherited transmission revenue requirement, I is new-load annual incremental transmission cost, and s is new covered load's share of billing demand. Ordinary payment is s(B₀ + I). The baseline is max[s(B₀ + I), I]. Credited AND payment is sB₀ + I. A is AND minus that baseline; B is AND minus I. Incremental receipts are credited out of the shared revenue requirement. A equals the reduction in other customers' bills. There is no existing-AND overlap deduction.

Growth presets and two cost sliders

Demand grows geometrically from 300 TWh in 2026 to 600 TWh in 2030. From 2031 onward, choose Low (1% annual growth), Standard (2%), or High (4%). Standard preserves the central case of 1,004 TWh in 2056. These are selected scenarios anchored to near-term studies, not published 2056 forecasts. At 80% load factor, energy becomes billing MW; 75% of new demand is treated as effectively chargeable. New demand begins in 2027; pre-2030 entrants are charged prospectively from 2030. Other billing demand stays at 829 GW. No terminal value beyond 2056 is counted.

Incremental annual cost equals assigned capital × [40-year capital-recovery factor at 5% real + 1.5% O&M], or 7.3278% of capital annually. The capital slider measures attributable shared-network investment; dedicated facilities, unrelated projects, funding credits and refunds must be reconciled in actual tariffs. The pooled higher-of calculation is a national approximation; regional aggregation can overstate additional receipts.

The national rate anchor, and PUDL review

EIA AEO2026 Table 54 reports a 2025 transmission-service price of 1.8851 cents/kWh and sales of 4,069.3 TWh, implying $76.71bn of transmission expenditure. Dividing by 829 GW of utility planning peaks gives $92.53/kW-year in 2025 dollars, or $95.08 after EIA's 1.02754 price adjustment to 2026 dollars. The slider rounds to $95.1/kW-year. This ratio uses national expenditure and demand totals, rather than an unweighted utility-rate average. It is a national NITS-equivalent cost proxy, not an observed national NITS tariff average: planning peaks differ from tariff billing determinants and geographic scopes differ slightly.

We inspected the local PUDL v2026.10.0 FERC Form 1 asset, expense, revenue, depreciation and rate-base tables. These support cost checks, but do not supply complete national transmission revenue requirements matched to network-service billing peaks. Wheeling revenue, transmission O&M, and wholesale power-purchase demand are not substitutes for that matched dataset.

Putting the ITC on a 30-year basis

JCT JCX-46-21, November 19, 2021, page 2, scores the House-passed BBB transmission ITC at $11.279bn in nominal FY2022–2031 cash flows. Its direct-pay outlays are already included. To build the comparison, each historical annual amount is divided by 1.025^(year − 2026). That produces $10.558bn in constant 2026 dollars. We repeat this real ten-year profile three times: $31.674bn over 2027–2056. The 2.5% normalization is an assumed common price convention, not observed historical CPI. This is not an official 30-year score, a forecast of the current ITC bill, an estimate of 30-year benefits from one ten-year credit, or a federal offset from BAAJA.
